Section 1: ACLS Resuscitation, Electrophysiology & Advanced Antiarrhythmic Kinetics

1.1 Electrophysiologic Mechanisms & Vaughan Williams Classification

Your heart beats because of electrical signals (called action potentials) that travel through heart cells. These electrical signals are created when microscopic gates—called ion channels—open and close to let charged particles like sodium, potassium, and calcium flow in and out of the cells:

  • Sodium channels act like a fast start switch, letting sodium in to quickly jump-start the electrical signal.
  • Calcium channels let calcium in to keep the signal going and help the heart muscle contract.
  • Potassium channels let potassium out to reset the electrical signal so the heart cell can relax and get ready for the next beat.

Antiarrhythmic drugs are medications used to fix irregular heartbeats. They work by blocking specific channels to do one of three things:

  • Stop heart cells from firing electrical signals on their own too quickly (suppressing automaticity).
  • Slow down how fast the electrical signal travels through the heart (decreasing conduction speed).
  • Increase the "cool-down" time a heart cell needs before it can fire another electrical signal (prolonging the refractory period).

1.2 Resuscitation Antiarrhythmics in Cardiac Arrest & Refractory Ventricular Fibrillation

During pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT) or Ventricular Fibrillation (VF) refractory to initial shocks and epinephrine:

  • Amiodarone: Exhibits all four Vaughan Williams mechanisms. Primary acute effect in cardiac arrest is Phase 0 sodium channel blockade and beta-blockade.
  • Cardiac Arrest Dosing: 300 mg IV/IO rapid bolus; second dose 150 mg IV/IO for persistent VF/pVT.
  • Perfusion Dosing: 150 mg IV over 10 minutes, followed by 1 mg/min continuous infusion for 6 hours, then 0.5 mg/min for 18 hours.
  • Clinical Pearl: Solvent formulations (polysorbate 80) can cause severe hypotensive vasodilation; non-solvent formulations (captisol-based) or slow infusions mitigate this risk.
  • Lidocaine: Class IB agent that selectively binds inactivated fast I_"Na" channels in depolarized, ischemic ventricular myocardium.
  • Dosing: 1.0–1.5 mg/kg IV/IO first dose; repeat 0.5–0.75 mg/kg IV/IO at 5–10 minute intervals to a maximum dose of 3.0 mg/kg. Continuous maintenance infusion at 1–4 mg/min.
  • Advantage: Does not induce systemic vasodilation or myocardial depression to the degree seen with amiodarone.

Section 2: Rate vs. Rhythm Control in Acute Atrial Fibrillation with RVR

2.1 Acute Rate Control Pharmacotherapy

(Diltiazem (Calcium Channel Blocker)

  • How it works: Blocks calcium gates in the heart's central electrical gatekeeper (the AV node). This slows down how fast electrical signals pass through and gives heart cells more time to rest between beats.
  • Dosing: Give 0.25 mg per kg of body weight injected into the vein over 2 minutes (a typical first dose is 15 to 20 mg). If needed, give a second dose of 0.35 mg per kg (usually 25 mg) after 15 minutes. Follow up with a continuous IV drip at 5 to 15 mg per hour.
When NOT to use it (Contraindication): Do not give to patients with severe heart failure or a very weak heart pump (ejection fraction under 40%), because it weakens how strongly the heart muscle squeezes.

Metoprolol Tartrate (Beta-Blocker)

  • How it works: Blocks adrenaline and stress signals going to the heart's beta-1 receptors to slow the heart rate down.
  • Dosing: Give 5 mg as a slow IV injection over 2 minutes. Can be repeated every 5 minutes up to a maximum total dose of 15 mg.

Digoxin

  • How it works: Blocks a specific pump in heart cells, which stimulates the body's natural "rest-and-digest" nerve (vagus nerve) to slow electrical signals passing through the heart's central gatekeeper.
  • When to use it (Indication): Used to slow down a fast heart rate in patients with sudden, severe heart failure and low blood pressure who cannot safely take calcium channel blockers or beta-blockers.

2.2 Rhythm Control & Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Emergency

  • Procainamide: Class IA antiarrhythmic. First-line agent for hemodynamically stable monomorphic VT and pre-excited atrial fibrillation (WPW with AF).
  • WPW Mechanism: Slows conduction down the accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent) and AV node.
Critical Safety Warning: AV nodal blockers (Adenosine, Diltiazem, Verapamil, Beta-blockers, Digoxin) are strictly contraindicated in WPW with Atrial Fibrillation. Blocking the AV node forces all atrial impulses down the unhindered accessory pathway, precipitating Ventricular Fibrillation.
  • Procainamide Dosing: 20–50 mg/min IV infusion until arrhythmia is suppressed, hypotension ensues, QRS widens >50%, or max dose of 17 mg/kg is reached. Maintenance infusion: 1–4 mg/min.

Section 3: Rapid-Acting Intravenous Antihypertensives in Hypertensive Emergencies

Hypertensive emergency is defined as severe blood pressure elevation (typically >180/120" mm Hg" ) accompanied by acute target-organ damage (aortic dissection, acute coronary syndrome, acute renal failure, intracranial hemorrhage, hypertensive encephalopathy).

RAPID-ACTING IV ANTIHYPERTENSIVE MATRIX

3.1 Clevidipine & Nicardipine Kinetics

  • Clevidipine: Ultra-short-acting dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker supplied as a 20% lipid emulsion. Metabolized by blood and tissue esterases, making its clearance completely independent of renal or hepatic function.
  • Key Advantage: Ultra-rapid onset and offset allow precise, minute-to-minute blood pressure titration without overshoot hypotension.
  • Nicardipine: Highly selective vascular calcium channel blocker that reduces SVR without depressing cardiac contractility or expanding venous capacitance.

3.2 Sodium Nitroprusside Toxicity (Cyanide & Thiocyanate)

Nitroprusside releases nitric oxide (NO) directly, dilating both arterioles and venules.

  • Metabolic Degradation: Each nitroprusside molecule contains 5 cyanide groups. Hemoglobin converts nitroprusside to cyanmethemoglobin and releases free cyanide.
Toxicity Clinical Picture: Prolonged high-dose infusions (>2" mcg/kg/min" for >24-48" hours" ) or renal/hepatic impairment cause cyanide accumulation, poisoning cytochrome c oxidase in mitochondria and producing severe uncompensated lactic acidosis, altered mental status, and cellular hypoxia.
  • Antidote Protocol: Administer Sodium Thiosulfate (enhances rhodanese conversion of cyanide to thiocyanate) or Hydroxocobalamin (binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin / Vitamin B12).

Section 4: Venous Thromboembolism: Fibrinolytic vs. Anticoagulant Strategies in PE

Risk stratification in acute Pulmonary Embolism (PE) determines the balance between bleeding risk and hemodynamic restoration.

4.1 Parenteral Anticoagulation Regimens

  • Unfractionated Heparin (UFH): Binds antithrombin III (AT-III), potentiating inactivation of thrombin (Factor IIa) and Factor Xa.
  • Bolus: 80 units/kg IV bolus; Maintenance: 18 units/kg/hr continuous infusion titrated to aPTT (1.5–2.5x control) or anti-Xa levels (0.3-0.7" IU/mL" ).
  • First-Line Choice: Preferred when systemic fibrinolysis or surgical embolectomy is anticipated, due to its short half-life (30-90" minutes" ) and complete reversibility with Protamine Sulfate (1 mg protamine per 100 units UFH).
  • Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (Enoxaparin): Higher ratio of anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity (3:1).
  • Dosing: 1 mg/kg SC every 12 hours (or 1.5 mg/kg SC daily). Reduce to 1 mg/kg SC once daily if "CrCl"<30" mL/min" .

4.2 Systemic vs. Catheter-Directed Fibrinolysis

  • Alteplase (recombinant Tissue Plasminogen Activator / r-tPA): Converts clot-bound plasminogen to plasmin, degrading fibrin matrices.
  • Massive PE Regimen: Alteplase 100 mg IV continuous infusion over 2 hours.
  • Cardiac Arrest / Impending Arrest Regimen: 50 mg IV rapid bolus over 2 minutes; repeat 50 mg bolus in 15 minutes if ROSC is not achieved.
Absolute Contraindications to Systemic Fibrinolysis:
  • Prior intracranial hemorrhage at any time.
  • Known structural cerebrovascular lesion or malignant intracranial neoplasm.
  • Ischemic stroke within the preceding 3 months.
  • Active internal bleeding (excluding menses).
  • Suspected aortic dissection or acute severe facial/head trauma within 3 months.
